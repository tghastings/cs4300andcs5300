Each sprint is two weeks long. Make sure that you work through both weeks of the sprint to achieve your tasks.

\section{Sprint Planning}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item At the beginning of the sprint, meet with your group to do \textbf{backlog grooming} and \textbf{prioritization}. Mark stories that are MVPs (minimum viable product).
    
    \item Begin the Sprint by looking over your backlog. Add stories or divide stories as needed---some of this may include playing \href{https://www.atlassian.com/blog/platform/scrum-poker-for-agile-projects}{Planning Poker} in your team to determine points and need for division. Record the changes in GitHub.
    
    \item Meet with your customer to identify the stories they want you to implement. Prioritize and confirm with them (if you can).
    
    \item The number of stories that you will complete each sprint depends on the difficulty of the stories. Select as many stories as you hope to get done---if you don't meet your expectations, that's okay, but each person should have a story/stories to work on. You will balance the workload between each of the sprints. At least two larger stories should be implemented and fully tested each sprint. Pairs of students may work on features that are especially large.
    
    \item Move the selected tasks from Backlog to the current Sprint.
    
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Note:} Keep the FIRST and SMART principles in mind---it is fine to divide stories and tasks so that they are achievable and measurable. MVPs should be prioritized. We only have 4 iterations, so pick what's really important! It is good to have a few extra MVPs/non-MVPs assigned to the sprint that can be ready to be assigned and moved on as initial tasks are completed.

\section{Development Requirements}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item All stories selected for a sprint should be assigned to a main person and include unit/integration tests.
    
    \item Follow the \textbf{Red-Green-Refactor} process. You don't need to worry about refactoring yet (end of semester).
    
    \item You are responsible for \textbf{full stack development}. In other words, you are responsible for building both the front end and the back end of your SaaS application and performing the associated integration and unit level testing.
    
    \item Communication and the use of branches will be key. Push, pull, and merge to main frequently. Protections have been placed on the main branch, so approval must be given for your merge to main to go through. \textbf{Work early} so that your branch is submitted in time for approval.
    
    \item As you finish tasks, move them along to completion in your project management tool.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Demo Expectations}

For each sprint demo, you must:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Share the URL to your production deployment so we can follow along
    \item Tell us about your application and why you're making it
    \item Go through GitHub Projects and show us what work you completed based on user story
    \item Demo the work you completed on the production environment showing the feature in production and showing us the test cases used to test each requirement
    \item Get customer feedback
\end{enumerate}

\section{Submission Requirements}

\subsection*{On Canvas (1 person)}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Submit your GitHub repository address
    \item Submit your application's production URL
    \item Submit a short report of what was implemented and tested. You may have not completed everything---include that in your report discussing what you will do.
    \item If your plan changed since your last submission, explain why
    \item If you are having issues with some of your tests, document why
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{On GitHub}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Make sure that all project files including your tests and code are committed and pushed to GitHub on the main branch
    \item \textbf{All} students should be pushing to GitHub. Make sure your name is associated with your GitHub pushes (\href{https://help.github.com/articles/setting-your-username-in-git/}{learn more}) so that you are not penalized for not contributing
    \item \textbf{Tag your git repository revision} with ``sprint\textit{i}'' where \textit{i} is the sprint number
    \begin{itemize}
        \item To tag the current revision: \texttt{git tag -a sprint1}
        \item \textbf{Warning:} You must explicitly push your tags with the command \texttt{git push --tags}. Before you submit, please make sure that we can checkout the tag---you can try this from the command line and also check the GitHub website to double check that the tag is there.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{On GitHub Projects}
Ensure project management tools are updated, cards are moved to done, and acceptance criteria has been developed for each card as gherkin scenarios. Be prepared to demo these scenarios during class. 

\subsection*{On Slack}

\subsection*{AI Documentation}
You must document in your project README where and when you got help from AI: the tool you used, what it helped with, and how you used the result. You do not need to include transcripts or chat logs.

\subsection*{Peer Evaluations}
Complete your peer evaluations! The activity opens immediately after the sprint deadline and has multiple stages to complete. See Section~\ref{sec:grading} for how they count.

\section{How Your Sprint Is Graded}\label{sec:grading}

Your team succeeds or struggles together, but your own engineering work is graded on its own. Every sprint you get a \textbf{team score}, an \textbf{individual score}, and points for \textbf{peer feedback}.

\subsection*{The formula}
\begin{center}
\fbox{\parbox{0.9\linewidth}{\centering
\textbf{Sprint grade} $=$ Peer feedback (12) $+$ 88 $\times$ (\textbf{65\%} Team $+$ \textbf{35\%} Individual)}}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Peer feedback (12 pts):} you earn these by completing every stage of the Kritik peer evaluation. They are the same 12 points listed in each sprint's rubric.
    \item \textbf{Team score:} the remaining items in that sprint's rubric (tests, coverage, CI, deployment, and so on). Everyone on the team gets the same team score.
    \item \textbf{Individual score (out of 30):} how much real engineering \emph{you} contributed this sprint. See below.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Example.} Your team earns 80 of its 88 team points (90.9\%), and you earn 24/30 individually (80\%). You complete Kritik (12/12).\\
Sprint grade $= 12 + 88 \times (0.65 \times 0.909 + 0.35 \times 0.80) = 12 + 76.6 = \mathbf{88.6}$.

\textbf{Minimum contribution.} If your individual score is below 15/30, a strong team score cannot carry you to a passing sprint grade. Dr.~Hastings reviews every case like this personally.

\subsection*{Your individual score (30 points)}

\begin{center}

\begin{tabular}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{4.2cm} c >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{9.3cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Category} & \textbf{Pts} & \textbf{What earns the points} \\
\midrule
Engineering substance & 10 & Meaningful, non-trivial work you implemented. A feature that spans model, view/API, and UI scores high. A button, header, or styling change alone scores low. CI/CD, deployment, security, AI integration, and test infrastructure all count as real engineering. \\
Delivered impact & 8 & Your work is merged to \texttt{main}, integrated, and working in production by the sprint tag. Unmerged work gets credit for effort, not delivery. \\

\midrule
\textbf{Total} & \textbf{30} & \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{You need both code and tests for full credit.} Implementation alone, or testing alone, cannot earn 30/30.

\textbf{We don't count activity.} Commits, lines of code, number of pull requests, and story points help us understand the work, but they are never turned into points. One substantial, well-tested feature beats fifty tiny commits.

\subsection*{What we look at}
A working feature is not enough by itself. We follow your work from start to finish:
\begin{center}
\textbf{Plan} $\rightarrow$ \textbf{Own} $\rightarrow$ \textbf{Build} $\rightarrow$ \textbf{Test} $\rightarrow$ \textbf{Review} $\rightarrow$ \textbf{Merge} $\rightarrow$ \textbf{Deploy}
\end{center}
That trail comes from GitHub: the cards assigned to you on your Project board, your pull requests, your code and tests, CI results, code reviews, and what made it to production. If you did the work but none of it shows up there, we can't give you full credit for it.

\subsection*{Make your work visible}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Assign yourself} to the cards you own on the Project board, and keep their status up to date.
    \item \textbf{Link every pull request to its card or issue} (for example, write ``Closes \#12'' in the PR description).
    \item \textbf{Put your tests in the same pull request} as the feature they test.
    \item \textbf{Push to GitHub under your own name.} If you pair program, add a \texttt{Co-authored-by:} line to the commit so your partner gets credit too.
    \item \textbf{Review your teammates' pull requests} and leave real comments.
    \item \textbf{Work across the whole sprint,} not in one push the night before.
\end{itemize}
Don't try to game the grader. If you follow good engineering practice all sprint, the evidence for a strong score will already be there.

\subsection*{Kritik peer evaluations}
Kritik is used in two ways:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Completion earns the 12 peer-feedback points.} Whether your reviews are kind or critical doesn't change your score. Just be fair and specific.
    \item \textbf{Your reviews give us context GitHub can't show}, such as pairing, debugging, helping a teammate get unstuck, or integration work. Answer this question: \emph{``Is there anything about this sprint that the GitHub history alone would give the wrong impression about?''}
\end{enumerate}
You are not grading your teammates. Positive context can raise someone's individual score when GitHub under-credits them. Negative feedback never lowers a score by itself; it only leads us to take a closer look at the GitHub evidence. What teammates write about you stays confidential.

\subsection*{Confidence}
Along with your individual score, your feedback includes an \textbf{evidence confidence} percentage. It tells you how clear the GitHub trail was. For example, it drops if your commits are hard to attribute or your cards aren't linked to your pull requests. It is \emph{not} a measure of how good your work was, and it does not change your grade. Low confidence means Dr.~Hastings looks at your work more closely.

\subsection*{Life happens}
If illness, a family emergency, or another serious circumstance affects your sprint, contact Dr.~Hastings as soon as you can. Depending on the situation, Dr.~Hastings may excuse your individual score (your grade becomes the team score), extend your deadline, or assign equivalent engineering work. Your team's expected scope is adjusted to match. Personal details are never written into your feedback.

\subsection*{Your feedback}
After each sprint, you will get a private written report explaining your individual score, what was strong, and what would raise it. It never compares you to your teammates. Your official grade is in Canvas.
